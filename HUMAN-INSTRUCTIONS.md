# Skillit brand guide

**Version 1.4 · October 2026**
**Last updated:** 2026-10-08
**Last synced with LLM-INSTRUCTIONS.md:** 2026-10-08

Use this guide when making a sales presentation, writing an email, planning a campaign, or reviewing work for Skillit. It covers the everyday decisions. You don’t need to learn the design system’s technical details to use it.

## Start with the worker

Skillit’s story is about skilled people finding work that recognizes their craft. Contractors are the customers; craft professionals are the subject. Show their expertise, choices, and real working lives.

Our guiding principle is the test: “Technology should make humans more essential, not more expendable.”

**Do**
- Respect people’s judgment and control over their careers.
- Describe the work and the practical benefit.
- Follow the writing rules below and the imagery rules in Photos and product images.

**Don’t**
- Don’t make technology the hero.
- Don’t describe people as inventory or suggest software decides for them.
- Don’t promise that AI will replace skilled work.

For example, say “Choose the right job for you.” Don’t say “We place you in open jobs.”

## Keep the page simple

Leave room around the content. Space, consistent left edges, and clear headings help people see what belongs together. A page does not need a box around every paragraph.

Use a larger heading to show what matters most, then readable supporting text. Keep related content aligned, including the logo, headings, and buttons.

**Do**
- Use open space to separate sections.
- Use white cards when content needs its own area.
- Keep the approved rounded cards and fully rounded buttons.

**Don’t**
- Don’t add divider lines, lines beneath headings, table gridlines, or shadows.
- Don’t fill open space with shapes, blobs, or illustrations.
- Don’t center text, including titles and button labels.

Useful chart lines and underlined links are allowed. They explain data or identify a link; they aren’t page decorations.

## Use color for a reason

Canvas is the warm off-white page background. Plaster is white, used for cards and separate content areas. Iron is the dark color for ordinary text. Keep these as the foundation; Iron is not a page background.

Traffic Cone is our orange. Use it for the main action, selected choices, and the visible outline that helps someone see which control they are using. More than one orange element is fine when each has a job. This is not an orange quota.

Give each section one dominant call to action: the button or link you most want someone to use. Other actions should look secondary. Primary buttons use dark Iron text on orange, not white text.

Status colors have specific meanings:
- Toolbox red means an error or a destructive action.
- Hard Hat yellow means a warning.
- Bottle Glass green means success.
- Painter’s Tape blue means information; it also identifies links.

Use a familiar icon and a clear label with a status. For example, a warning should say what needs attention, not just show a yellow symbol. Keep the message area white with dark text.

**Do**
- Use the approved palette and existing treatments.
- Underline text links.
- Ask for a readability check when colored text or symbols are hard to see.

**Don’t**
- Don’t use color alone to explain a status or a chart.
- Don’t use status colors as page backgrounds, card fills, or decoration.
- Don’t add gradients or invent new colors.
- Don’t use orange for body text or color headings just to show their rank.

Some approved treatments have known readability limitations. A label does not fix low contrast. Get design review rather than changing the color yourself or calling the treatment accessible without checking.

## Choose readable type

Use Work Sans for headings, paragraphs, buttons, and other words written for people. Use JetBrains Mono for machine data such as IDs, timestamps, distances, and data references. Don’t use it for ordinary paragraphs or headings.

Write in sentence case: “How it works” and “Join for free,” not “How It Works” or “JOIN FOR FREE.” Keep normal capitals in names and acronyms such as Skillit, OSHA, and HVAC. Preserve the capitalization in direct quotes and information entered by users. The approved logo stays as supplied.

**Do**
- Align text to the left.
- Use size, bold emphasis, and space to make the reading order clear.
- Use smart quotes and curly apostrophes in finished copy.
- Check line breaks at the size people will actually read.

**Don’t**
- Don’t use extra-thin type or substitute another font.
- Don’t shrink text to squeeze in more content.
- Don’t italicize a whole quote or paragraph for decoration.

If a heading wraps awkwardly, shorten it or ask design for help. Keep the text readable rather than forcing it onto one line.

## Use the approved logo

The primary logo is the horizontal lockup: the mark and Skillit name together. Use the mark alone when space is too tight for the full logo. Never use the name alone as a logo.

Choose an approved file from the [logo folder](./assets/logo/). Keep its proportions, spacing, and colors intact. Leave clear space around it and keep it large enough to recognize.

**Do**
- Use the supplied logo file rather than typing the name.
- Use the approved dark logo on a light background by default.
- Use an approved PNG only when the platform won’t accept SVG.

**Don’t**
- Don’t stretch, rotate, redraw, recolor, or stack the logo.
- Don’t add outlines, shadows, or effects.
- Don’t put the mark inside a circle, box, or shield.
- Don’t use retired logos or place the logo over a busy photograph.

The approved boot-tread pattern is the only decorative graphic allowed. Keep it neutral and in header or footer areas without content. Never put it behind text, controls, or photos, or enlarge it into a main illustration.

## Use icons that already belong to Skillit

Choose from the [approved icon folder](./assets/icons/). Use an icon to explain an action, a trade, or a status, not to fill space. Keep its approved shape and colors.

Don’t replace Skillit icons with a different icon library, emoji, or a newly generated drawing. Don’t use the legacy archive for new work. If the icon you need isn’t available, ask design; a planned icon is not a delivered asset.

## Photos and product images

Use genuine images of skilled people and real work. Choose approved design-team imagery; source new stock photography from Adobe Stock with generative AI excluded. Show workers at eye level or below, with their hands and work visible where possible.

Project photography can show a site from above. That permission does not extend to looking down on a worker. Separate authentic photos can form a simple grid collage; don’t merge them into a fictional scene.

**Do**
- Choose candid, dignified work and natural color.
- Use genuine product screenshots.
- Keep collage photos separate, with consistent lighting.
- Ask design to check any text placed over a photo.

**Don’t**
- Don’t use AI-generated people, scenes, or product images.
- Don’t invent a product screen or present a feature illustration as a screenshot.
- Don’t use staged smiles, fake work, or costume-like safety gear.
- Don’t add color grading, dramatic filters, feathered edges, or replacement backgrounds.
- Don’t overlay icons, graphs, or interface elements on a photo.

A dark overlay is allowed only where text needs help staying readable. It is not a filter for standalone photos. If a worker’s background is removed, place the worker directly on Canvas.

Full photography rules: [rules/photography.md](./rules/photography.md).

## Presentations

Start with an approved presentation template supplied by the team. This repository does not include a presentation template; ask for the current one rather than assuming a file here is a deck.

Keep one main point on each slide. Use a clear heading, a short explanation, and only the evidence or image needed to make that point. Follow the template’s text styles rather than turning website sizes into a fixed slide point scale.

**Do**
- Keep headings and content left-aligned.
- Leave generous margins and space between ideas.
- Check readability at presentation size, including chart sources.
- Split a crowded slide into separate slides.

**Don’t**
- Don’t inherit divider lines, borders, or shadows from a presentation theme.
- Don’t add decorative colors or make every item a card.
- Don’t shrink footnotes until they are unreadable.

## Email

Use at least 16px for all readable email text. This includes signatures, contact details, addresses, legal links, copyright, captions, and source references. Footers are not an exception.

Use Work Sans where supported and check the result in the email tools your audience uses. Keep text and buttons left-aligned. Let contact details wrap on a phone rather than shrinking them.

Give each section one main next step. Keep button wording consistent: use “Join” for account creation. Don’t add an arrow to a button label or present “Start hiring” and “Get hired” as competing primary buttons.

For worker newsletters, make the next step practical and easy to find. For employer newsletters, lead with a verified outcome and the evidence behind it.

## Charts and numbers

A chart should answer a question clearly. Give it a title that says what it shows, explain the timeframe, and include the approved source, date, group measured, and definition of the measure.

Use a white Plaster card with dark readable labels. Keep bars flat and square-ended. Simple dark charts are a good starting point; approved colors can distinguish categories when that helps the reader. Label those categories directly or provide a legend, with cues beyond color.

Worker availability uses blue and job openings use orange when shown together. These colors describe the data; they don’t mean the chart is clickable.

**Do**
- Show the context needed to interpret a number.
- Use only chart lines that help people read the data.
- Keep sources readable, including the 16px minimum in email.

**Don’t**
- Don’t use donut, radar, spider, or 3D charts.
- Don’t add shadows, outer chart boxes, or vertical gridlines.
- Don’t turn an old example into a current claim.

Full chart rules: [rules/data-viz.md](./rules/data-viz.md).

## Write plainly

Write like someone who knows the work and respects the reader. Use everyday words, concrete details, and short sentences. Let the facts carry the point. Don’t imitate workers’ speech or add excitement to make a weak claim feel stronger.

**Do**
- Say “connect,” “match,” or “find.”
- Say “craft professionals” first in employer-facing copy; “workers” may follow.
- Say “you,” the trade, or “craft professionals” when addressing workers.
- Say “members” for people who have joined.
- Say “platform” or “network” for Skillit.
- Use short, genuine, attributed quotes when they add useful perspective.

**Don’t**
- Don’t say “serve,” “support,” “represent,” or “proud” in brand copy.
- Don’t describe our people as products, customers, or a labor marketplace.
- Don’t lead with “AI-powered” or proprietary technology.
- Don’t say “revolutionize,” “disrupt,” “world-class,” or “thrilled to announce.”
- Don’t add exclamation marks, jargon, stacked adjectives, or empty comparisons.
- Don’t use AI as the source of an idea or the final reviewer.

For example, say “Getting the right worker to the right job site.” Don’t say “Optimizing the talent acquisition pipeline.”

Match the channel. A website explains clearly and offers proof. Worker messages give a short, respectful next step. Sales materials and employer messages focus on precise outcomes. Insights explain a grounded argument. LinkedIn can carry an opinion in the first person, backed by evidence. News should explain the impact on people, not just list features.

Sam is an AI assistant tool, not a person. Sam helps coordinate work and find information; the human decides. Use simple, direct language for Sam, without emojis or exclamation marks. Don’t invent a personality or promise an unverified capability.

## Check claims before publishing

Before reusing a number, get an approved source, the date, the group and timeframe measured, the exact definition, and the person responsible for approving the claim. A number in an old deck or on a public page is not enough.

The “72 hours” claim is blocked from reuse. Published copy measures profile setup to interview; older copy measures search to interview. Those are different starting points. The claim owner must resolve the definition with supporting data before either is reused. Don’t relabel it as time to hire or fill a role.

“Free” applies only to craft professionals. Confirm current approved terms; don’t imply employers or the whole platform are free. Verify product capabilities, relationships, permissions to use partner logos, and testimonials before publishing. Proposed features are not shipped features.

## Final check before sharing

- [ ] The worker’s expertise and choices stay central.
- [ ] The wording is plain, appropriate to the audience, and free of hype.
- [ ] Claims have approval and enough context; the blocked timing claim is absent.
- [ ] Text is left-aligned, sentence case, and readable at the final size.
- [ ] Each section has one dominant next step; orange has a purpose.
- [ ] Backgrounds are Canvas or Plaster; statuses have clear labels.
- [ ] There are no decorative lines, shadows, gradients, or added colors.
- [ ] Logos and icons are approved and unchanged; imagery is genuine.
- [ ] Email text, including the footer, is at least 16px.
- [ ] Links work, line breaks hold up, and the finished piece has had a human review.
