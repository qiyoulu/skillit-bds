# Audience — Skillit BDS
**Last updated:** 2026-08-17
---
## Default rule
When in doubt, write for the worker first. The worker is the subject. The contractor is the customer. These are not the same thing.

Beyond both of them, the audience Skillit is ultimately speaking to is the American public. The brand tells a story about skilled hands being recognized — about dignity of work, about the tradesperson or tradeswoman whose expertise should mean something. Every surface should pull in that direction.
---
## Audience 1: Craft workers
### Who they are
- Skilled tradespeople: ironworkers, welders, carpenters, electricians, pipefitters, heavy equipment operators, and other craft labor classifications.
- Range: early-career workers building their first verified profile → experienced journeymen with deep NCCER certification stacks.
- On the job site or between jobs — often reading on a phone in the field, not at a desk.
- Many have been let down by systems that didn’t understand their work: job boards that treated a welder like a data entry clerk, staffing agencies that treated them as a number.
### What they need (functional)
- Visibility — to be seen and findable by the right employers.
- Speed — to find the next job without months of searching.
- Dignity — to have their skills recognized accurately, not reduced to a keyword.
- Control — to choose the right job, not just any job.
### What they need (emotional)
- To feel respected as a professional. Not as a “resource” or a “labor unit.”
- To feel that this tool was built for them, not just for the contractors who hire them.
- Confidence that their skills and certifications will be seen and valued.
- Simplicity — they do not want to spend their off-hours managing a complex profile.
### How to write for craft pros
- Short sentences. They’re reading on a phone, often with limited attention.
- Direct and practical. “Complete your profile to get found faster.” Not “Optimizing your profile increases your discoverability.”
- Respect the craft. Name the trade. Acknowledge the skill. Never generic “workers.”
- Never condescend. Never explain what a two-by-four is. They already know.
- Warm and human — not transactional, not corporate.
- First words should orient them: what action, what benefit, in that order.
### Worker-facing surfaces
- App (primary) — home screen, job feed, profile, notifications, certifications
- Email — job alerts, application updates, profile completion nudges
- Onboarding — profile creation flow

## Audience 2: Contractors (hiring teams)
### Who they are
- General contractors, specialty trade contractors, and their hiring teams (HR, project managers, superintendents).
- Range: small specialty shops hiring 5-10 workers/year → Tier 1 GCs (Mortenson, DPR, Kiewit) with enterprise hiring pipelines.
- Decision-makers who have experienced the pain of not finding workers when a project starts.
- Data-driven when given data. Relationship-driven when they’re not.
### What they need (functional)
- Speed — to find and verify qualified workers before the project start date.
- Confidence — to know the worker will show up and deliver.
- Efficiency — to reduce the recruiter time spent on sourcing and screening.
- Scale — to build a reliable bench of workers across trades and regions.
### What they need (emotional)
- To feel that Skillit understands construction, not just software.
- To trust the data — that a “verified” profile actually means something.
- To feel in control — the tool surfaces people, the human still decides.
- To see ROI language they can use with their own leadership.
### How to write for contractors
- Outcome-first. Lead with the result: “Hire qualified welders in Dallas before Monday.”
- Precise and data-backed. 4x productivity, 2x interviews, 3x retention — use real numbers.
- Infrastructure framing. Skillit is not a vendor — it is the hiring operating system.
- Professional but not corporate. Our direct register applies here too.
- ROI language is acceptable at the enterprise level: “guaranteed positive ROI,” “cost per hire,” “time to fill.”
### Contractor-facing surfaces
- Dashboard (primary) — job posting, worker pipeline, matches, analytics
- Email — match alerts, role updates, account reports
- Sales materials — one-pagers, decks, proposals
- Investor materials — market size, three systems, Act 1/2/3 narrative

## Audience 3: Investors (secondary)
### Who they are
- Tier-one VCs, infrastructure investors, strategic partners considering the fundraise.
- Evaluating market size, team credibility, competitive moat, and unit economics.
- Familiar with tech but may not know construction deeply.
### What they need
- The market size argument: \$600B+ infrastructure build cycle, workforce as the bottleneck.
- The insight: it’s not a shortage, it’s an access problem — and access problems are solvable with data.
- The moat: 200K+ verified workers, proprietary skill data, three interlocking systems.
- Founding credibility: not tech tourists, but builders who built tech.
- The arc: Act 1 (construction) → Act 2 (training pathways) → Act 3 (every physical industry).
### How to write for investors
- Infrastructure framing throughout. “Operating system,” not “platform.” “Data layer,” not “app.”
- Counterintuitive framing on the labor gap (5% gap, 9-in-10 pain — explain the delta).
- Ambitious but grounded. “Scale the world’s craft” followed by “here’s how we’re doing it.”
- Executives speak for themselves in investor contexts — use his voice directly.

## Audience 4: Press (secondary)
- Journalists covering construction-tech, future of work, AI, and the physical economy.
- Looking for a story, not a press release.
### What they need
- A strong lead: Fraser’s biography, or the counterintuitive labor gap stat.
- A human angle: the two-by-four test, the worker who got hired 10x faster.
- A broader thesis: AI making humans more essential, not more expendable.
### How to write for press
- Story first, product second. Never lead with features.
- The founder story is the brand voice for press. If an industry veteran wouldn’t say it in an interview, don’t put it in a press release.
- Avoid jargon. Explain every technical term immediately after using it.

## Two vantage points (same system, different camera)
The Skillit platform is one system. But the worker and the contractor look at it from opposite sides.
| Element | Contractor view | Worker view |
|---|---|---|
| Job post | “Posted this role, here are my matches” | “This role matches your certs” |
| Worker profile | “Here’s who this worker is and what they’ve done” | “Here’s how you appear to contractors” |
| Verification badge | “This worker is vetted” | “Your skills are recognized” |
| Sam | “Sam, find me ironworkers in Houston” | “Sam, show me welding jobs starting next month” |
When generating UI copy, content, or any surface — identify the vantage point first. The same data, described from the wrong camera angle, will feel off.
